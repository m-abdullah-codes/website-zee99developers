# Zee99 Lifestyle — Page Direction & Copy

*Goes at `docs/direction.md`. The brief for the build: concept, section architecture, voice, and draft copy. Numbers live in `docs/facts.md` and `data/inventory.json` — never here.*

---

## Part 1 — The reframe

The most useful thing to fix before designing anything: **this page's reader is not a stranger.** They have already seen the renders, already asked the price, already said "send me the details." A website seduces. This page has a different job — **remove doubt and make the reader feel intelligent for considering it.**

That inverts the usual emotional arc. On a normal project site the peak is the render. Here the peak should be **the numbers.** Everything before them is context; everything after them is proof. The money section should be the most beautifully made thing on the page.

Second reframe, and this is the structural trick: **a brochure is finite and a webpage is not.** You can hold a brochure and feel how much is left. A scrolling page feels bottomless, which is exhausting on a long read. So give the page *covers*. An opening frame and a closing frame that clearly bookend it, and a persistent, almost-invisible indicator of position — a hairline, a section mark in a corner, a page count. Make finiteness a felt quality. That alone will make it read as a document rather than a site.

---

## Part 2 — Three concepts for the spine

Pick one and let it govern the whole page. Don't blend them.

### A. **One Evening** ← recommended

Every render in the asset folder is dusk or after dark. The exteriors are blue hour, the rooftop is night, the interiors are warm-lit. The developer's own headline is already *Experience Every Evening*.

So: **the page is one evening.** Scroll is time passing. Within a light theme this reads as a drift in temperature and weight rather than a fade to black — cool and bright at the top, warming through the interiors, settling into something quieter and denser by the closing frame. Nothing announces it. It's felt, never read.

Why it works: it gives every animation a *reason*, which is the difference between sophisticated motion and decorated motion. And it makes a long page feel like one continuous shot instead of a stack of blocks.

Opening: the cinematic video, full bleed, silent, **no text for two full beats.** Then one line resolves — small, low in the frame, not centered. Confidence is quiet. The video is the strongest asset here; the worst thing you can do is delay it with a word animation.

### B. **Facing**

The floor plans label every edge: *FACING SPORTS COMPLEX* / *FACING RESIDENCE*. That's a gift. Every apartment is defined by which way it looks — units 01–04 to the sports complex, 05–08 to the residences — and the whole page can be built on that binary. A split that runs its full length, one side the view out, one side the life in. Structural, architectural, derived entirely from the drawings rather than imposed on them.

### C. **The Ledger**

Anti-brochure. Cold open on the number — Rs 18,000 per square foot — before a single image. Then earn it backwards. The spec sheet literally uses the phrase *radical transparency*; this concept takes it at its word. Risky, and only right if the sales lead is confident. But for warm leads who've already seen the pretty pictures, it's the most disarming thing available.

**Typography note (any concept):** avoid the high-contrast serif on cream. It's become the default premium look and reads as templated. This building is aluminium, double-glazing, laminate and concrete — a precise, slightly cold grotesque for display against a genuinely warm body face sits closer to the subject. Set the numbers in something with proper tabular figures; there are a lot of them and misaligned digits will undo the whole premium read.

| 13 | Questions | FAQ |
| 14 | Closing frame | Approvals, registration, contact |

Two judgement calls, overrulable:

- **Builder before the numbers.** Earn the right to ask for three million rupees before asking. The alternative — builder *after* — answers the doubt at the moment it peaks. Both defensible.
- **Inventory and progress adjacent.** Same emotional beat: *this is real and it is moving.*

## Part 4 — Copy

Voice: declarative, short, unhurried. No exclamation marks. No *luxury redefined*, no *your dream awaits*, no *nestled in the heart of*. Numbers used as poetry. Where options are given, they're different registers — pick by how bold the client is.

### 00 — Opening frame

> **ZEE99 LIFESTYLE**
> Bahria Town Lahore, Sector B

One line, after the video has breathed:

- *Facing the sports complex. Backing a fifteen-year record.*
- *Eight floors on a corner that was already the best one.*
- *Sector B, from the eighth floor.*

### 01 — The proposition

**A finished address, not a promise of one.**

Sector B is already built. The roads are laid, the schools are running, the market is busy, the hospital is open. What was missing was somewhere to live in the middle of it.

That is the whole idea. Eight floors on a corner plot facing the Safari Sports Complex — two commercial, six residential — in a neighbourhood that stopped waiting for its future a decade ago.

*Stat strip, set small and wide:*
`8 FLOORS` · `48 APARTMENTS` · `23 SHOPS` · `CORNER PLOT` · `LDA & TMA APPROVED` · `30 MONTHS TO POSSESSION`

---

### 02 — The address

**You will not need the car.**

*(alt: **Everything already here.** / **Nothing about this location is a projection.**)*

Most projects sell a location by describing what is coming. Here is what is already there.

Padel courts, tennis, badminton and cricket across the road. Safari Mall next door. Qartaba Masjid on the corner. Safari Park and the zoo across the 80-foot road. Green Valley Supermart, the money exchanger, Bahria's largest mobile market, the study centres, and Bahria Hospital — all inside the same few blocks.

*Map caption:* The plot sits on a 40-foot road, between a 60-foot double road and the Safari Sports Complex.

> **Design note:** don't use a stock Google Maps embed. The bird's-eye render is far better than anything Google will return — clean, labelled, roads at their real widths. Use it as the primary and put a live map behind a small secondary control for anyone who wants to navigate.

---

### 03 — Residential, the idea

**Built small, on purpose.**

None of these apartments are large. That is deliberate.

A 500-square-foot home facing the sports complex, finished properly, costs less than a plot two sectors out — and can be lived in from the day it hands over. Every foot here is spent on the parts of a home you actually use. There is no formal drawing room nobody sits in.

---

### 04 — Specification

**Premium is a word. This is the list.**

*(alt: **What is behind the walls, named.**)*

*Lead-in:* Most brochures say premium and stop. Below is what is fitted in every apartment, semi-furnished, before a single piece of your own furniture goes in.

Set the brand list as a spec table, not prose — it's more convincing as data. Closing line: *Chosen for service records in this market, not for the logo.*

---

### 05 — The typical floor

**Eight homes to a floor. One five-foot corridor.**

A corner plot means the building has two good faces instead of one. Four apartments on every floor look out at the sports complex, four at the residences behind — there is no dead elevation. Lift and stair sit at the centre, so no home is more than a few steps from either.

All six residential floors are identical. What you see here is what you get on any of them.

---

### 06 — The three homes

**Studio · 380 sq ft**
**One room, thought all the way through.**
A separator wall, not a curtain. Attached bath, full kitchen, a wardrobe run along the wall, back ventilation. Nothing in it is decorative, which is why it lives larger than the number suggests.

**1 Bedroom · 500 sq ft**
**A bedroom that closes. A terrace that opens.**
The lounge runs out onto a private terrace. The kitchen is open but properly equipped — a kitchen you cook in, not one you photograph. Attached bath, full wardrobe wall.

**2 Bedroom · 800 sq ft**
**Room for a family, on the best corner of the plate.**
Two bedrooms, both with attached baths, set at opposite ends so nobody walks through anyone else's room. A dining table that seats six without moving the sofa. A laundry room. A private terrace facing the sports complex.

*Repeating caption device across all three, from the drawings themselves:*
`← FACING SPORTS COMPLEX` ... `FACING RESIDENCE →`

**Gallery.** Each type gets its interiors — 5 scenes for the Studio, 11 for the 1 Bed, 13 for the 2 Bed. This should be the most considered interaction on the page: obvious to operate, generous at full size, and genuinely pleasant on a phone with one thumb. Scene numbers are non-contiguous and carry no order — sequence by what reads best.

---

### 07 — The building

**Experience every evening.** *(keep — it's the developer's line and it's good)*

A cinema on the roof. A BBQ lounge beside it. Solar above both.

Card access at the door, a reception desk behind it, cameras on every floor and a fire system running through the building. Room service, if you want it.

**Split the amenities into two groups.** *In the building* and *across the road* — the developer's own list mixes them, and presenting a nearby hospital as a building feature is exactly the sort of thing that costs trust on a page whose only job is to build it. Separating them costs nothing and reads as honesty.

**These are serviced apartments.** Say it plainly, and say what it means: Zee99 finds the tenant, handles the maintenance, and transfers the rent to your account. For anyone buying to let, that is the whole proposition and it currently appears nowhere in the developer's material.

---

### 08 — Commercial

**Two floors of shopfront on the corner everyone walks past.**

Footfall here isn't speculative. The sports complex is across the road, Safari Mall is next door, and forty-eight households live directly upstairs. The plot fronts a 40-foot road off a 60-foot double road, with parking on both sides.

**The two floors are different products. Don't flatten them.**

**Ground — nine shops behind a ten-foot arcade.**
305 to 486 square feet, opening onto a covered arcade that runs the length of the frontage. Shade in June, cover in monsoon, and enough depth to put a display outside the door. This is the floor you take if the street is your customer.

**Lower Ground — small-format retail.**
Thirteen units from 180 to 390 square feet, plus a kiosk, on six-foot corridors. The 180-foot cells are the size that works for phone accessories, opticians, tailoring, cosmetics — the trades already doing volume a few blocks away at Bahria's largest mobile market. Lower entry, lower rate, faster to fill.

> **Design note:** the floor plates are good drawings — clear, labelled, with every shop numbered and sized. Sell from them directly. Commercial inventory belongs on the plan itself rather than in a table, so a buyer can see which unit is where and what's still free in one look. That's also what makes the arcade frontage legible, which no table will do.

---

### 09 — The numbers ← the peak

**Rs 18,000 a square foot. Every apartment, every floor.**

Three unit types. Three prices. One rate. No premium for a better view, no discount for a worse one — 380 feet or 800, the arithmetic is identical.

*Sub-line:* About a fifth down. Thirty-six months to pay. Thirty months to possession.

**The line nobody has used yet:** possession lands at month 30 and the payment plan runs to month 36. **The keys arrive six months before the last installment does.** Phrase it precisely — a large payment falls due *at* possession and six monthly installments follow it — but the point holds, and it deserves to be a headline rather than a footnote.

Also worth saying out loud: every schedule sums to its total exactly. It is a small thing that quietly implies a much larger one.

**No calculator** — the client dropped it. But a payment **timeline** is not a calculator: it computes nothing the buyer inputs, it just draws the plan honestly. Thirty-six months laid horizontally, every payment marked at its month, possession flagged at month 30. Switching unit type re-plots the same shape at a different scale, which proves the flat rate more convincingly than the sentence does. This is the obvious candidate for the page's one orchestrated animated moment.

Commercial pricing is its own block: Ground at one rate, Lower Ground at another, and the same 20 / 30 / 20 / 10 / 20 split on every unit.

*Footnote:* Prelaunch prices move with construction progress, demand and supply.

---

### 10 — The builder

**The last one finished early.**

Zee99 Arcade's structure is complete. Possession is eight months out with fifteen months still to run on its payment plan — ahead of schedule, which in this market is the only credential that counts.

Before it: Takwa Center, delivered. Safari Apartments, delivered.

Fifteen years. Fifty-plus homes. Six commercial projects. Two high-rises. A hundred-odd owners who have already been through this process with them.

> Flag: *100+ Happy Customers* inside a five-star laurel is the weakest visual in the developer's deck — the same graphic every developer uses. If three named owners with a sentence each can be sourced, cut the laurel and run the quotes. If not, cut the laurel anyway and let the delivered-projects list carry it.

---

### 11 — Live inventory

**What is left.**

*Updated [date from the data file].*

Residential reads as a floor-by-floor stack. Commercial reads against the floor plates. Sold and booked units go quiet; available stays lit. Tap for type, size, floor, price and status.

Four states, and the labels matter — *Available*, *Reserved* (token paid), *Booked* (on installments), *Sold* (paid in full). Publishing the distinction between a token and full payment is unusually candid and worth not smoothing over.

*Sub-line:* The rate on this page is a prelaunch rate. It holds until construction says otherwise.

> Every count on this section is derived from the data file, never typed. The page has to stay true when someone edits a status.

---

### 12 — Site progress

**Photographed on site, [date].**

No renders below this line. Every image is the plot as it actually stands, dated.

A photograph grid, in date order. Seed it with the approval and registration milestones so it reads as a timeline from the first day rather than an empty frame, and let photographs fill in as they come.

---

### 13 — Questions

Answerable from the material:

**Is the project approved?** LDA and TMA approved. Zee99 Developers is FBR-registered.

**When is possession?** Thirty months. The payment plan runs thirty-six.

**Is the apartment furnished?** Semi-furnished. Kitchen, wardrobes, doors, art walls, bathroom fittings, hood and stove, smart lighting and motorised curtains are all fitted. Furniture is yours.

**Can Zee99 rent it out for me?** Yes. Their team finds the tenant, handles maintenance, and transfers rent to your account.

**Will the price change?** Yes. Prelaunch rates move with construction progress and availability.

**How many apartments are there?** Six identical residential floors, eight homes each. Forty-eight in total, plus twenty-three commercial units across two floors.

Still needs client input — and these are the ones buyers actually ask:

- What am I buying — registry, sub-lease, or allotment? Transferred when?
- Monthly maintenance charges?
- Is parking allotted, and included?
- Can I resell before possession, and what's the transfer fee?
- What happens if I'm late on an installment?
- Discount for full or accelerated payment?
- What does the rental programme charge, and is there a guaranteed period?
- Who's the contractor? Structural and seismic spec?
- What's covered by the "at possession" payment — utility connections, meters?

### 14 — Closing frame

**Zee99 Lifestyle**
Sector B, facing the Safari Sports Complex.
Approved, registered, and under way.

*Approval marks: FBR · LDA · TMA*

Then the contact block. Not a CTA — the back of a brochure. Address and WhatsApp, set by a typographer rather than a marketer: no button styling, no form, no urgency. Without it the page dead-ends and the reader has to go hunting through WhatsApp for whoever sent it, which is the exact moment you lose them.

## Part 5 — Still open

Not blocking, but worth chasing:

1. **Payment trigger months.** Which month does the "on structure" payment fall? Which six months carry the half-yearly payments? The timeline can be drawn without them but it's better with them.
2. **Rental programme terms.** Management fee, guaranteed period, who pays for what. This is the single biggest unexploited selling point on the page and it currently has no detail behind it.
3. **Named owners.** Three sentences from real buyers of Takwa Center or Safari Apartments would outperform every trust device on the page.
4. **Site photographs.** Placeholders are in until they arrive.
5. Confirm the office address spelling — *Nishter* vs the more common *Nishtar*.

---

## Part 6 — Two things to resist

**Don't overspend on scroll animation.** The instinct toward sophisticated motion is right, but the fastest way to make a premium page feel cheap is to have every block fade up on entry. Pick **one orchestrated moment** — the payment timeline is the obvious candidate — build it properly, and let everything else move quietly or not at all. Restraint reads as expensive. Scattered motion reads as a template.

**Don't let it become the website again.** This goes to WhatsApp, which means it opens on a phone, one-handed, probably in the evening, probably while the sender waits for a reply. Design the phone version first and treat desktop as the adaptation. And keep it finishable — if it takes more than four or five minutes to get through, it has stopped being a brochure.
